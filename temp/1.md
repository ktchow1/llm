# Piecewise Linear Regression for Yield Curve Fitting

This note describes a weighted, regularized piecewise-linear regression for fitting a bond delta-yield curve. It also shows how the fitted curve is used to adjust noisy bond mid-prices and how the mathematics maps to the implementation.

## 1. Market data and curve parameters

Assume that the market contains `N` tradable bonds. For bond `n`, the inputs are maturity, best bid, best ask, previous close price, and DV01:

$$
\left\{T_n,\ b_n,\ a_n,\ c_n,\ d_n\right\},
\qquad n=0,\ldots,N-1.
$$

The delta-yield curve is parameterized at `M` predefined tenor points. Its control-point vector is:

$$
\Delta Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta y_{\tau_0} & \Delta y_{\tau_1} & \cdots & \Delta y_{\tau_{M-1}}
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.
$$

Here, delta yield means the difference between the current yield and the previous closing yield at a given tenor. Typical control tenors are 0, 1M, 3M, 6M, 1Y, 2Y, and so on.

For any maturity `T`, the fitted curve value is written as:

$$
\Delta y(T\mid\Delta Y).
$$

## 2. Convert bond prices to observed delta yields

An observed bond price can be converted to a delta yield by solving the internal-rate-of-return equation, for example with bisection or Newton-Raphson.

When the current price is sufficiently close to the previous close, a first-order Taylor approximation is cheaper. Define the observed mid-price and price change by:

$$
m_n = \frac{a_n+b_n}{2},
\qquad
B_n = m_n-c_n.
$$

Using DV01 as the local price-to-yield sensitivity, the approximation is:

$$
m_n \approx c_n+d_n\Delta y_{T_n},
\qquad
\Delta y_{T_n}\approx\frac{m_n-c_n}{d_n}.
$$

The regression is performed in delta-yield space, not absolute-yield space. This preserves each bond's previous-close relative-value offset; Section 8 explains why this matters.

## 3. Piecewise-linear interpolation

Suppose bond (n) matures between adjacent curve tenors (\tau_j) and (\tau_{j+1}). Define its interpolation weight by:

$$
\tau_j\le T_n\le\tau_{j+1},
\qquad
\mu_n=\frac{T_n-\tau_j}{\tau_{j+1}-\tau_j}.
$$

Its fitted delta yield and fitted price change are then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta y(T_n\mid\Delta Y)
  &= (1-\mu_n)\Delta y_{\tau_j}+\mu_n\Delta y_{\tau_{j+1}},\\
\widehat B_n
  &=d_n\Delta y(T_n\mid\Delta Y).
\end{aligned}
$$

A common market-liquidity weight is the inverse squared bid-ask spread:

$$
w_n=\frac{1}{(a_n-b_n)^2}.
$$

The weighted least-squares objective is:

$$
\min_{\Delta Y}
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
w_n\left[
d_n\left((1-\mu_n)\Delta y_{\tau_j}+\mu_n\Delta y_{\tau_{j+1}}\right)-B_n
\right]^2.
\tag{1}
$$

## 4. Matrix form

Let `A` be the design matrix with `N` rows and `M` columns, `B` the observed price-change vector, and `W` the diagonal weight matrix:

$$
B=\begin{bmatrix}B_0&\cdots&B_{N-1}\end{bmatrix}^{T},
\qquad
W=\operatorname{diag}(w_0,\ldots,w_{N-1}).
$$

Instead of writing a large sparse example matrix, each row of `A` can be defined exactly. For a bond whose maturity lies between adjacent tenor points, row `n` has only two nonzero entries:

$$
A_{n,k}=
\begin{cases}
d_n(1-\mu_n), & k=j,\\
d_n\mu_n, & k=j+1,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore the objective becomes:

$$
\min_{\Delta Y}
(A\Delta Y-B)^T W(A\Delta Y-B).
\tag{2}
$$

## 5. PCA transformation and regularization

Apply an orthonormal PCA rotation `T` to transform delta-yield grid values into risk-factor coordinates:

$$
\Delta Y'=T\Delta Y,
\qquad
\Delta Y=T^{-1}\Delta Y',
\qquad
A'=AT^{-1}.
$$

The transformed factors typically begin with curve level, tilt, and curvature, followed by higher-order factors.

Let `P` be a diagonal penalty matrix. The first three factors can be left unpenalized, while progressively larger penalties suppress higher-order factors:

$$
P=\operatorname{diag}(0,0,0,p_3,\ldots,p_{M-1}),
\qquad
0\le p_3\le\cdots\le p_{M-1}.
$$

The regularized objective is:

$$
J(\Delta Y')=
(A'\Delta Y'-B)^T W(A'\Delta Y'-B)
+(\Delta Y')^T P\Delta Y'.
\tag{3}
$$

Setting its gradient to zero gives the normal equations and the optimal parameters in one derivation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla J
  &=2(A')^TW(A'\Delta Y'-B)+2P\Delta Y'=0,\\
\left((A')^TWA'+P\right)\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}
  &=(A')^TWB,\\
\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}
  &=\left((A')^TWA'+P\right)^{-1}(A')^TWB.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

In production code, solve the linear system rather than explicitly forming the inverse when the available matrix library permits it.

## 6. Adjusted bond prices

The main output need not be the yield curve itself. Given noisy bond mid-prices, the fitted model can produce adjusted prices that average out cross-sectional observation noise.

For a training-set bond, let `A-prime[n]` denote row `n` of the transformed design matrix. Its adjusted mid-price is:

$$
m_{n,\mathrm{adjusted}}
=A'_n\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}+c_n.
$$

For any other bond with maturity `T`, close price `c`, and DV01 `d`, construct a transformed one-row design matrix using the same interpolation rule. Then:

$$
m_{\mathrm{adjusted}}
=A'_{\mathrm{row}}\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}+c.
$$

## 7. Low-latency implementation

Factor the weight matrix into two identical diagonal square-root weight matrices. Define the reusable weighted regularized pseudo-inverse:

$$
R=\left((A')^TWA'+P\right)^{-1}(A')^T\sqrt W.
$$

The calculation separates naturally into two paths:

- Slow and rare: recompute `R` when the design matrix, weights, PCA transformation, or penalty changes.
- Fast and frequent: update the result whenever market price vector `B` changes.

The per-tick path is:

$$
\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}=R\sqrt W B,
\qquad
m_{\mathrm{adjusted}}=A'_{\mathrm{row}}(R\sqrt W B)+c.
$$

### Code correspondence

The mathematical notation above treats observations as rows of `A`. The framework code uses the transposed convention, so dimensions and multiplication order must be converted accordingly.

#### `FSimpleLinearCurveMid::initialize()`

| Code variable | Mathematical quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `m_wmat` | `T inverse` | Inverse PCA transformation |
| `m_trans` | `A prime` | PCA-transformed design matrix |
| `m_trans2` | `A-prime transpose * A-prime + P` | Unweighted regularized Gram matrix |
| `m_transinv` | `inverse(m_trans2)` | Inverse unweighted Gram matrix |

#### `FSimpleLinearCurveMid::updateRotation()`

| Code variable | Mathematical quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `m_midwght` | `sqrt(W)` | Diagonal square-root weight matrix |
| `m_trans_scaled` | `sqrt(W) * A-prime` | Weighted, PCA-transformed design matrix |
| `m_trans2` | `A-prime transpose * W * A-prime + P` | Weighted regularized Gram matrix |
| `m_transinv` | `inverse(m_trans2)` | Inverse weighted Gram matrix |
| `m_rotation` | `R` | Weighted regularized pseudo-inverse |

#### `FSimpleLinearCurveMid::operator()`

| Code variable | Mathematical quantity | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `m_res` | `R * sqrt(W) * B` | Optimized risk-factor parameters |
| `m_tmp` | `A-prime * m_res` | Fitted price changes |
| `value()` | `m_tmp + c` | Adjusted prices |

The initialization function first computes the unweighted inverse and then calls `updateRotation()` to compute the weighted inverse. The first calculation is slightly redundant if viewed only as an inverse calculation. Its main purpose is to obtain a determinant under the all-ones-weight condition. That reference determinant is retained for detecting ill-conditioning as `W` changes during the functor's lifetime.

## 8. Parameter space and bond relative value

The optimization applies two coordinate changes:

1. Absolute yield to delta yield.
2. Delta yield to PCA risk factors.

The PCA rotation is useful because level, tilt, and curvature have economic interpretations and usually capture the dominant yield-curve movements. Penalizing higher-order factors also produces a smoother fitted curve.

Delta yield is useful because bonds do not all trade exactly on one absolute yield curve. Two bonds with similar maturities can have different implied yields because of bond-specific relative value, also called basis in the framework model. A bond trading above comparable peers is said to trade rich; one trading below them is said to trade cheap.

Examples of relative value include:

- Sovereign or country basis at the same maturity.
- On-the-run versus off-the-run premium.
- Cheapest-to-deliver premium.
- Special-repo premium.
- Asset-swap spread.

Conceptually, the market price decomposes as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{bond price}
&=\text{yield-curve price}+\sum\text{relative-value components},\\
&=A'(R\sqrt W B)+c
+\text{country basis}
+\text{on-the-run premium}\\
&\quad+\text{CTD premium}
+\text{repo premium}
+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
$$

Some firms trade these relative values while neutralizing exposure to broad yield-curve risk. In the framework, alpha-price models can be interpreted as models of these basis components.

## 9. Compact formula summary

For convenient transfer into a small number of Confluence LaTeX boxes, the complete calculation can be summarized in two blocks.

Model construction:

$$
\begin{gathered}
B_n=\frac{a_n+b_n}{2}-c_n,
\qquad
W=\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{(a_n-b_n)^2}\right),\\
\Delta Y'=T\Delta Y,
\qquad
A'=AT^{-1},\\
R=\left((A')^TWA'+P\right)^{-1}(A')^T\sqrt W.
\end{gathered}
$$

Per-tick fitting and price adjustment:

$$
\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}=R\sqrt W B,
\qquad
m_{\mathrm{adjusted}}=A'_{\mathrm{row}}\Delta Y'_{\mathrm{opt}}+c.
$$
